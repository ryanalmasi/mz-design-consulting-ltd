A municipal review cycle is measured in weeks. Two or three cycles is a meaningful
portion of a development schedule, and each one is a period where nothing downstream
can proceed.

The useful observation is that the comments causing those cycles are largely
predictable. They cluster.

## What a reviewer is actually doing

A reviewer is checking that the submission demonstrates compliance with the
municipality's standards, that it is internally consistent, and that it is complete
enough to be assessed at all.

That last point drives more returns than engineering disagreement does. A reviewer
who cannot find the basis for a number cannot approve it, and the fastest available
response is to ask for it. From the outside this reads as a rejection on the merits.
Usually it is a request for information that was in the engineer's file but not in
the submission.

## The deficiencies that recur

**Design criteria not stated.** The submission uses a runoff coefficient, a design
storm, a rainfall dataset, a roughness value. Where did each come from? If the
criteria are not stated, every downstream number is unverifiable.

**Pre- and post-development comparison not like for like.** The controlled quantity
is the difference between two modelled conditions. If the two are modelled with
different catchment boundaries, different methods, or different assumptions about
what existed before, the difference is not meaningful. Reviewers check this
specifically.

**Catchment boundaries that do not account for external drainage.** Water arriving
from upslope of the site is part of the system whether or not it is in the model.

**Inconsistency between documents.** The report says one thing, the drawings show
another, the calculations use a third value. This is the single most common
consistency comment, and it usually comes from a design revision that propagated into
some documents but not all.

**Missing or unstated datum and benchmark.** Elevations without a stated datum cannot
be checked against anything.

**Drawings that are not self-consistent.** Grades that do not close, spot elevations
that disagree with contours, sections that do not match the plan, details referenced
but not included.

**Outlet and overflow not addressed.** Detention designs that establish the storage
volume but do not demonstrate the release rate at each stage, or that do not show
what happens in an event exceeding the design storm.

**Erosion and sediment control treated as a formality.** ESC plans that show a silt
fence around the perimeter and nothing about construction sequencing, stockpile
management, dewatering or stabilisation are routinely returned.

**Servicing conflicts.** Proposed utilities that clash with each other, with existing
infrastructure, or with the grading — most often at crossings, where required
separation is not demonstrated.

## The pre-application conversation

The highest-leverage step available is a conversation with the reviewing authority
before the design is finalised — on the approach, the design criteria, the
submission contents, and anything about the site that is unusual.

It is inexpensive. It takes a meeting. And it converts the most expensive category of
comment — a fundamental disagreement about approach, discovered after the design is
complete — into a design input.

It is particularly worth doing when:

- the site has an unusual constraint, or discharges somewhere unusual
- you intend to use an approach the standard does not explicitly contemplate
- the site straddles a jurisdictional boundary, or drains into another jurisdiction
- the project is on a schedule that cannot absorb a second cycle

## Alberta and British Columbia are not interchangeable

Both provinces have provincial legislation governing water and land development, and
both delegate a great deal to the municipality. The practical consequence is the
same in both: **the governing requirements are the local ones**, and they differ
between neighbouring municipalities within the same province.

A design approach that was approved in one municipality is evidence that the approach
is sound. It is not evidence that it complies with the standard next door. Beyond the
municipal layer, provincial approvals may be triggered by works affecting a
watercourse, by the volume of water diverted, or by the nature of the development —
and the applicable triggers, thresholds and processes differ between the two
provinces.

The working assumption that holds up is: confirm the governing standard for the
specific site, in the specific municipality, at the start of every project, and do
not carry assumptions across a boundary.

## Building the submission to be reviewed

A submission that is easy to review gets reviewed faster. In practice:

- **State the criteria up front**, with their sources, in one place.
- **Show the calculation**, not only the result.
- **Make the documents agree.** Before issuing, check the report, drawings and
  calculations against each other for the values that appear in more than one.
- **Include what the checklist asks for**, in the order it asks for it, where the
  municipality publishes one.
- **Address the known issues explicitly.** If something about the site is unusual,
  explain it in the submission rather than leaving the reviewer to discover it.
- **Respond to comments completely.** A response that addresses four of five
  comments guarantees another cycle.

None of this is engineering. It is the difference between one review cycle and three.
